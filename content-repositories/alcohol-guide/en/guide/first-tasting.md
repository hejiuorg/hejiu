# Give the first sip a moment

Tasting is not an exam. Its first job is to help you notice what you like, why you like it, and what you want to compare next time.

## Start by noticing

Look at the colour and clarity, then smell. Familiar fruit, grain, flowers or spices are useful comparisons. You do not need expert vocabulary to begin.

Smell twice: a light pass first, then a little closer. With a high-strength drink, there is no need to bury your nose in the glass.

## Separate your impressions

Notice sweetness, acidity, bitterness and texture. Write down what you sense before connecting those impressions. Different people may describe the same drink differently.

A simple note can include:

- the product, or a style name such as [baijiu](ref:baijiu);
- temperature, glass and whether ice was added;
- one line each for aroma, palate and finish;
- what you liked or disliked.

## Let preference be personal

“I like that fruit aroma” is a useful note. Tasting helps you understand your own preferences; it does not have to become an exam.

## Continue exploring

- Comparing two products in the same style is more useful than memorising every term at once.
- Keep subjective impressions separate from verifiable facts: ABV, region and process come from sources; aroma and texture come from your observation.
