# Time in a cup

Drink connects farming, seasons and people coming together. In early Chinese texts, the first questions are often not “what does this drink taste like?” but “when is it brewed, when is it drunk, and with whom?”

## Spring wine and the autumn harvest

The Book of Songs, in “Seventh Month” from the Odes of Bin, says:

> In the eighth month we strike down the jujubes, in the tenth we reap the rice. We make this spring wine, to help the aged live long.

The lines place harvesting, brewing and a wish for long life in one sequence of time. The “spring wine” here cannot be mapped directly onto a modern product. What it shows is a place for drink within seasonal and ritual life.

## When technique is written down

Qimin Yaoshu, compiled by Jia Sixie in the Northern Wei period, records various methods for making qu, using it and brewing. Once technique enters a text, experience can be copied, compared and taught. It is not the fixed recipe of a modern region, but it gives a traceable documentary line into traditional brewing.

## Cups in poetry

By the Tang dynasty, drink appears in poetry again. In Li Bai's “Bring in the Wine”, the line “When life gives a moment of triumph, take your joy; do not let the golden goblet face the moon empty” joins drinking, time and emotion. This essay quotes the four lines and cites the received Complete Tang Poems text in its references; no separate excerpt page is kept.

These lines are not advice about how much to drink. What is worth noticing is how drink becomes a language for time: after the harvest, among friends, under the moon, a cup carries meaning beyond consumption.
